Case study · Head of Risk, Compliance & Legal

Listen first. Fix what hurts most. Make risk the team Skip wants in the room.

Today risk sits with the COO, alongside everything else he owns — and it works. My job is to understand why it works, find where it won't scale, and build a function the business pulls in rather than routes around.

Said plainly

I'm not a credit specialist — your first line is, and it should stay that way. I bring payments, fintech, consulting and AML/CTF, and a habit of finding the yes inside a grey area. I'll learn credit from the people who do it here.

Your question 1

What does day one look like — what do you do in your first two weeks, before you change anything?

Top-down, bottom-up, then meet in the middle. No solutions yet.

Week 1 · Top-down

Start with Adam and the leadership: how do they see risk at Skip, what's the appetite, what worries them?

"Where do you feel exposed — and what breaks if one person leaves?"

Adam first: he has carried this. What does he hold, what keeps him up, what must not break?

Weeks 1–2 · Bottom-up

Then the people who run it: credit, credit ops, product and engineering, the broker team.

"Walk me through how this actually happens. Where do you work around the process?"

I sit with credit assessors too — that's how I learn credit here.

Week 2 · The written word

Only now the paper: the policy suite, the registers, the last BRC packs, the funders' review findings, the AML/CTF program.

Day 14 · Meet in the middle

Lay the three views over each other. Where they agree, it works. Where they disagree is where the risk is — and that's what answers your second question.

Your question 2

What are the three highest-risk gaps you would expect to find in a setup like this, how would you confirm them quickly, and how would you close them?

The gaps come out of that investigation — not into it.

i · Assumption vs workaround

What leadership believes happens, against what operations actually does.

ii · Shelfware

Policies that drifted from both views — and the compliance gaps that hide inside them.

iii · Risk becomes someone's job

Going from risk as the COO's side job to a dedicated function is a culture change, not an org-chart change.

The examples are patterns I've seen in other firms — not findings about Skip. The real three come out of day 14.

Your question 3

How would you sequence the first ninety days — what do you build now, and what do you deliberately leave until the book and the team are bigger? Why that order?

Compliance gaps first. The 20% that causes 80% of the pain.

The backlog on day 14

Everything feels urgent, and everything is someone's favourite. Taking it in the order it arrives is how a new function drowns.

Pareto

Sort by pain. A few issues carry most of it. Those are the focus; the long tail waits, on a list, with an owner.

The risk appetite sets the bar

What leadership told me in week one becomes the line. Above appetite: now. Within it: managed, monitored, later.

Where is the manual work?

A second lens: where people spend hours by hand. That's not always where the risk is — it's where automation pays back fastest. Low-hanging fruit, and the answer to question 4.

So, the ninety days

Understand, close the vital few, automate one thing. And a deliberate list of what waits.

Your question 4

Pick one control or process you would automate rather than staff? Walk us through how you would actually get it live: what you build versus buy, the scoped ask to engineering, what operations does differently, and how you make sure it is used rather than worked around.

Automate where there's a financial or client payback. Onboarding.

Today

Documents chased by email, details re-keyed, checks run one after another. Every hand-off is cost for Skip and friction for the customer and the broker.

Automated

Buy the commodity checks, build a thin layer that routes by risk. Low risk goes straight through; higher risk goes to a person, with the case already summarised.

Risk sets the dial

How much goes straight through is a risk-appetite decision, made with credit — not mine alone. Drag it.

Used, not worked around

Make the compliant path the fastest path. If the automated route is quicker than the workaround, nobody works around it.

Getting it live
The scoped ask to engineering

Two sprints. No new screens.

Build
Take the broker's submission as data, call the vendors in parallel, route on rules we agree.
Buy
ID verification, AML/CTF screening, bank-statement reading — commodities.
Output
A decision-ready case in the queue credit already uses.
Not now
A new portal. Machine learning.
What operations does differently
  • Stops re-keying and chasing; works only the cases that need judgement
  • Starts each case from an AI-drafted summary — evidence, not a decision
  • Records a reason on every override
  • Brokers see the status instead of calling for it
How I'd know it's used
  • Share straight-through, by broker and channel
  • Time to decision, and document requests per application
  • Overrides, and their reasons
  • Applications abandoned part-way

The payback is two-sided: lower cost per application for Skip, less friction for customers and brokers — and AML/CTF due diligence captured the same way every time, with the evidence kept.

Your question 5

What does the function look like at twelve months, and how would you know it is working? Include one thing you would be able to tell the Risk Committee that they cannot see today.

A culture that brings risk in, not one that routes around it.

How I work

Not a police officer. The question is never "can we?" but "how do we, safely?" — sometimes not the way it was first drawn.

A grey area

The law is rarely black and white. Same question, three answers.

Risk moves earlier

At launch, risk can only stop a product. At discovery, it can shape it. Over twelve months, risk moves up the product's life.

At twelve months

A small function the business pulls in: me, one hire triggered by volume, risk champions in credit ops and product, automation doing the repetitive work, external counsel for specialist credit opinions.

A broker submits a 98% LVR loan. The 2% deposit is a gift from the applicant's parents overseas. Settlement is in nine days, and the broker sends Skip a lot of business.
Grey-area register · entry
The issue
Gifted deposits from overseas family on high-LVR loans.
The readings
Decline as a category · accept without question · accept with proportionate evidence.
Our position
Accept, with a gift declaration, evidence the funds left the giver's account, and screening of the giver.
Why defensible
Proportionate, consistent across brokers, written down before the next case.
Residual risk
A broker steering gifted deposits — so we watch the share per broker.
Revisit when
One broker's share moves away from its channel.

Who found it.
